4 Methodology



Aylo has comprehensively re-assessed and revised its risk assessment framework inaccordance with Articles 28, 34, 35 DSA and the Guidelines. As part of its broaderrevisions, Aylo has integrated the minors-specific risk review under Article 28 DSA andsection 5 of the Guidelines into the broader systemic risk assessment it has conductedunder Article 34 DSA, as provided for in section 5, paragraph 23 of the Guidelines.To integrate the two frameworks in a meaningful way that allows for a consistent andholistic assessment of risks and mitigations, Aylo has established an overarchingmethodology that applies across both parts of the assessment. This holistic approachis complemented by additional tailored methodological components that are specificto the Article 34 DSA risk assessment on the one hand, and to the minors risk reviewunder Article 28 DSA and the Guidelines on the other hand.Through this integrated and transparent methodology, Aylo reinforces its commitmentto high standards of user protection, regulatory compliance, and responsible platformgovernance, consistent with the objectives of the DSA and the expectations placed onVLOPs with their heightened systemic impact.

4.1 Aylo’s holistic methodology for the risk assessment



Aylo employs a structured, multi-phased risk review cycle that reflects establishedindustry standards for systemic risk governance. This framework encompassessystematic risk identification, the assessment of inherent risks, a comprehensiveevaluation of existing mitigation measures, a subsequent assessment of residualrisks, and the determination of additional safeguards where necessary. Each phaseis designed to enable a holistic, evidence-based understanding of potential

15



systemic risks and to support continuous improvement in alignment with theobjectives of the DSA.

Aylo’s review and assessment of systemic risks is grounded in a holistic review ofhow risks and corresponding mitigations impact users online. Throughout itsoperation, Aylo’s overarching objective has been to present a safe onlineenvironment to its users. Online safety does not stop at the confines of any givenplatform. This is particularly true and impactful for adult content online, for a numberof reasons we set out in detail below and that have their origin in the combinationof the omnipresent availability of easily multipliable adult content and the veryspecific way users behave and guard their privacy and intimacy when seeking outadult content online.

Taking these market realities into account, the only viable approach for meaningfullyand sustainably protecting users is to identify, assess and mitigate systemic risksconnected to an adult content platform such as Pornhub holistically. Both foridentifying and assessing the risks associated with Pornhub and for assessing theneed for, and effectiveness of, existing and potential additional mitigation measures,Aylo considers, in the first place, their effects on the Pornhub service, as well astheir potential implications for the online ecosystem more broadly.

This holistic approach is not only fundamentally necessary to achieve effectiveprotection of users in relation to risks associated with the dissemination andconsumption of adult content online; it is also deeply grounded in the frameworkand objectives of the DSA to ensure a safe, predictable and trusted onlineenvironment, Article 1(1) DSA and Recital 9. On boththe level of systemic risks and on the level of effective mitigation measures, aholistic assessment is both prescribed by the DSA and factually without alternatives:



First, when identifying and assessing systemic risks, VLOPs are expectedunder the DSA to take into account the effects of any systemic risks originating from,or associated with, the VLOP service. By their very essence and definition, systemicrisks are those that have implications extending beyond an individual service andthat affect the wider online ecosystem and society at large. Risks that remain purelyconfined to the service itself are merely platform-specific rather than systemic.

While the DSA does not provide a standalone definition, the concept of systemicrisk is clearly reflected in the concrete examples set out in Article 34(1)(a)–(d) DSA.The risks enumerated there are, by their nature, not limited to effects confined tothe platform level, but encompass broader societal implications and impact. This isparticularly evident with respect to negative effects on civic discourse and electoralprocesses under Article 34(1)(c) DSA, which do never materialize solely within theconfines of any given online service.

This interpretation is further reinforced in Recital 76, where it is recognised that verylarge online platforms and very large online search engines may give rise to societalrisks that differ in scope and impact from those associated with smaller services.Taken together, these provisions make clear that systemic risks under the DSA areunderstood as risks extending beyond the platform itself and affecting the wideronline ecosystem and society in the EU at large.

Several other Recitals also emphasize this ecosystem-wide dimension. Forinstance, Recital 79 explicitly references broader 'safety online' and the shaping ofpublic opinion, while Recital 82 highlights the impacts on democratic processes,civic discourse, electoral integrity, and public security. Acknowledging this broaderscope, the DSA also encourages cross-platform cooperation, such as participationin codes of conduct and other self-regulatory mechanisms, see Recital 88.

Looking at specific examples of systemic risks, the European Board for DigitalServices (EBDS) in cooperation with the European Commission in its first report onthe most prominent and recurrent systemic risks as well as mitigation measuresinter alia flags risks stemming from the sale of "illegal, non-compliant, orunregulated medical products" on marketplaces. The EBDS notes that suchproducts - including counterfeit cosmetics, toys, and health remedies - create risksthat manifest in the physical world when consumers use these products (see page11 of the report, at https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/press-statement-european-board-digital-services-following-its-16th-meeting).

To give a nother stark and very real example: Research has shown that whereextremists are banned from mainstream social media, they migrate to otherplatforms, where the radicalization of the extremists rises (Tourkochoriti, 2023, p.144, https://cjil.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/2023-07/24ChiJIntlL129.pdf).

In the context of video-sharing services, systemic risks can similarly extend beyondthe service on which content is initially made available. Harmful or illegal contentmay not only reach users on a given platform but may also be further disseminatedthrough re-uploads, embedding, or cross-platform sharing, leading to broaderamplification across the online ecosystem. Where platform scale, design features,or dissemination mechanisms contribute to such amplification, these effects mustalso be taken into account when assessing systemic risks under the DSA.



Second, once systemic risks have been holistically identified and assessed at thenext level of countering the identified risks through effective mitigationmeasures, VLOPs again need to consider whether any given measure is effectiveand proportionate not only at the level of their own platform but also beyond itsconfines. Where the assessment of risks online places too narrow a focus,mitigation measures are bound to fail in an online environment that allows users tofreely move from one site to another without territorial limitations and other offlinerestraints, such as language and culture. And if any given measure is exclusivelyeffective on the platform, but directly and causally results in an increase inrisk for the same users off platform, the measure has failed. It is not effective,and therefore also cannot be proportionate. Ultimately, i t cannot be legallyowed since it does not further the objectives of the DSA but frustrates them.



Furthermore, when determining reasonable, proportionate, and effective mitigationmeasures, the weighing of all impacted fundamental rights is one of the key criteria,as also underscored in Recital 86. The effective protection of fundamental rightsalso necessitates a holistic approach that transcends immediate and platform-focused factors. To adequately address fundamental rights implications, thebroader ecosystem consequences of any mitigation strategy must be carefullyconsidered and weighed. Consequently, the DSA does not, and cannot lawfully,mandate measures that would merely improve fundamental rights protection for thespecific user group of one individual service, and only for the duration of their visiton that service – especially if such measures result in increased risks beyond theplatform, thereby reducing the overall level of protection online. Such measurescannot be deemed effective under Article 35 DSA, nor can they be proportionateand required by law.

And finally, this holistic understanding of systemic risks and mitigation measures isalso grounded in the requirements set out by the European Commission in theGuidelines. In particular, the Guidelines require providers to assess the “potentialpositive and negative effects on children’s or other users’ rights of any measure that

In line with the necessities of this holistic approach, when Aylo evaluates existingmitigation measures, as well as potential additional ones, Aylo assesses whethereach measure is capable of meaningfully addressing the relevant identified risk,and whether its scope and intensity are justified by the likelihood and severity ofharm. At the same time, Aylo undertakes a holistic balancing of all involved rightsand legitimate interests and evaluates potential adverse and unintendedconsequences. This includes whether a measure could create new risks or shiftrisks for minors and users off platform, whether it could disproportionately restrictlawful access to information or participation, and whether it could introduceavoidable adverse privacy or data protection impacts.

4.1.2 Assessment of inherent risks



The inherent risk represents the baseline risk score of each identified risk beforeany safeguards or mitigation measures are applied. It reflects the naturalexposure associated with a risk in its unmitigated state and forms the analyticalfoundation for Aylo’s broader risk-assessment methodology. Where the sameunderlying risk affects distinct groups in materially different ways (for example,viewers and victims), Aylo evaluates separate scores to reflect those differencesaccurately.

Given the robust mitigation measures that have been in place on Pornhub longbefore the entry into application of the DSA, the assessment of the inherent risk

21



for each risk category necessarily relies on an informed estimation. To determinea risk-score, Aylo applies a structured risk matrix evaluation, which incorporatesseveral key factors to ensure a nuanced and evidence-based assessment, andwhich was created by UNICEF to enable companies to identify, prevent andmitigate risks specifically for minors:



• Scope – the estimated number of users who could be affected bythe adverse impact.



• Scale – the severity of the actual or potential impact on affectedusers.



• Remediability – the extent to which those affected can berestored to their original or an equivalent position.



• Likelihood – the probability that the adverse impact will occur.

Wherever possible, Aylo bases these factors on quantitative and qualitativedata derived from the operation of its service. For instance, when a potentialrisk is directly linked to a specific product feature or user activity pattern, Ayloestimates the number of potentially affected users using relevant service-basedindicators.

4.2 Specific methodology for the risk review under Article 28 DSA



For the risk review under Article 28 DSA and the Guidelines, Aylo categorizes theidentified risks for minors based on the 5C typology of online risks to children. The5C typology has been developed by the OECD and provides a framework forcategorizing online risks to children in digital environments. Specifically, five areasof risks are described: Content (exposure to harmful material), Contact (harmfulinteractions), Conduct (engaging in or being subjected to harmful behavior),

Contract/Consumer (commercial exploitation), and Cross-cutting (overarchingrisks, such as to privacy and well-being).



OECD. (2021). Children in the digital environment - Revised typology of risks. p.7.

https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/children-in-the-digital-environment_9b8f222e-en.htm



Content risks arise from minors being exposed to hateful, age-inappropriate, orillegal material or disinformation. In this context, minors are recipients/viewers ofthe content. This can include exposure of minors to pornographic content, violentcontent or CSAM. For the purposes of this assessment, Aylo also includes the risksfor minors caused by appearing in CSAM within this category.



Conduct risks arise where a minor is the actor who engages in harmful behavioror is the victim of such behavior by peers. This involves the minor's own interactionsand contributions to the creating the risk. This includes, for example, cyberbullyingand sexting (in platform environments where direct user interactions are possible).



Contact risks arise from minors being subjected to harmful interaction with otherusers. This includes adults seeking to establish inappropriate relationships with

29



minors or peer-to-peer harassment. In this case, minors are the victims or recipientsof the actions. This includes cyber grooming or sex trafficking.



Consumer risks are related to the exposure of minors to illegal or inappropriateproducts or harmful marketing strategies, advertisers using minors' online datawithout informed consent, financial harm through online fraud or exploitation ofminors' lack of commercial literacy, and security harms like free download optionscontaining malware.



Cross-cutting risks are systemic risks that affect a child’s fundamental rights andwell-being across all categories. These include privacy risks, risks of using andbeing exposed to AI and other technologies, and risks to physical/mental health.For example, risks can result from increased data collection to determine a user'sage and verify their identities or from data breaches collected by AI-basedtechnologies.

o Websites that carry the RTA label display an RTA tag in theirHTML code. Operating systems, browsers, and parentalfiltering software recognize this tag and block access to thewebsite for users under the specified age limit.

10.2 Porn addiction



Porn addiction is often described as a problematic pattern of compulsive anduncontrolled consumption of pornographic material. This risk is only applicable toa subset of users and should not be assumed as a general or inherent outcome ofexposure.

However, “porn addiction” is not a scientifically established or formally qualifiedmedical condition. European clinical frameworks, including those of the WorldHealth Organization, do not recognise it as a distinct disorder, and there is noclear clinical consensus that it meets the criteria of an addiction in the same wayas substances or gambling. While some individuals may experience problematicor compulsive patterns of use, these are typically considered under broadercategories—rather than as a standalone, validated diagnosis—meaning the term“porn addiction” remains a contested and non-standard label in scientific andmedical literature.

There is, correspondingly, limited agreement within the psychological field that“porn addiction” constitutes a distinct, treatable condition. A number of studiessuggest that problematic porn use may instead reflect underlying issues such ascompulsive behaviour, anxiety, depression, or relational difficulties, rather than aprimary addiction.

For example, research discussed in Psychology Today – Is Porn Addiction Reallya Disorder? notes:



“The problematic porn or self-described ‘porn addiction’ use can be viewed moreas a symptom of deeper psychiatric issues and/or relational conflicts the personhas with others.”



Similarly, Psychology Today – Science Stopped Believing in Porn Addiction. YouShould, Too highlights that perceived addiction is often linked to moral or culturalconflict rather than clinical dysfunction:

“Having moral conflict over your porn use (PPMI) does turn out to be bad for you.But that's not because of the porn. Instead, higher levels of moral conflict overporn use predict higher levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and diminishedsexual well-being, as well as religious and spiritual struggle”
